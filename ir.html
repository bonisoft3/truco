Truco — engineering design

Compiled from brief.md (pinned above) by the studio team, onto the mecha cluster, the omnishell terminal, the sayt loop and the bayt build graph.

Overview

Truco runs local games on the device and online tables through the live cluster. Match keeps the local sitting; Round, Play and Held are terminal projections. The lobby, challenges and shared online game rows use the live data path.

Arena and lobby use hybrid machines. Their pure handlers fold game and lobby state into rows, and declarative regions render those rows. Presentation uses native bindings, CSS, timers and pure text renderers. The app declares no escape hatches (presentation contract).

One sitting: the two scores, the rules in force, the seats, the felt, and what the current hand is worth. It is the table's own row — the arena reads it as a singleton (status=eq.playing) and the pickers write it. The score is the dealer's to move (score-hand); the three pickers move nothing else (pick-variant, pick-seats, pick-theme). A match that reaches twelve is closed by status, not deleted, so the result stays on screen until the next one is asked for (decision-09).

Decisions

01 — nothing leaves the device, and the sitting outlives the tab. The brief asks for a game that owes the network nothing, and durability is the axis that buys it. The hand is tab: a round, its plays and the cards you hold are the sitting itself and have no meaning after it. The match is device, because a score you were in the middle of is worth finding again tomorrow, and that is one word of program rather than a different kind of app. Neither rung emits a table, trigger, publication, policy or migration; neither syncs. The cost is stated rather than hidden: there is still no cross-device anything, and a browser that forgets its storage forgets the score. The right cost for a bar game and the wrong one for a ledger.

02 — no terminal escape. The shared hybrid rules own the game; the arena fold projects the view into rows. Bindings and pure renderers draw them, and CSS owns motion. Country selection writes through a declarative chart before following its locale link.

03 — what you do reaches the table as a row. A click writes an intent — the game, the revision it was made at, what it asks — and touches the table's row so the fold wakes (decision-32). That is the store's one write path for the game, so a click made twice is one intent, a click on a stale table is dropped, and every step of a game is one the terminal recorded.

04 — no pipelines. The shared rules and hybrid folds calculate scores and trick verdicts. The terminal receives those results as ordinary row projections; online commands use the shared machine’s revision and command identity contract.

05 — the phase spine is a field, not markup state. Round.phase is one text column and the tracker is a region over it. The alternative — the screen keeping its own idea of the phase — puts the spine and the hand out of step exactly when a vaza resolves, which is the moment the tracker exists for.

06 — switching variant restarts the match. Paulista and Mineiro do not merely differ in manilhas; the ladder starts at a different rung, so a score carried across the switch would be a score no rulebook produced. Restarting is honest and the dropdown says so.

07 — a table turns a card up or fixes its manilhas, never both. Paulista and gaúcho turn a vira and read the rank above it. Mineiro and the Río de la Plata tables name four cards in the rules and turn nothing, so the round carries no vira and the strip that names one is not drawn — a slot showing a card nobody laid is what reads as a bug, and it is the reading the fixed-manilha player is most likely to make.

08 — the log is append-only. Play is never updated or deleted, not even between hands. It makes the hand auditable during play, gives the QA seat a surface it can drive assertions against, and removes a class of bug the store would otherwise hold: a rewritten log cannot be told from a misplayed hand.

09 — a finished match is closed, not deleted. status moves to over and the arena's singleton read stops matching it, so the closing scoreline stays on screen until a new match is asked for. Deleting would take the result off the table at the moment it is being read.

10 — every picker is the app's own dropdown. The brief demands no OS popup, and the reason is the design's: a native select menu is drawn by the operating system, and an OS menu on a felt table breaks the object the whole screen is building. The panel is the graduated omnishell--picker: selection is a machine over the match row, and hidden-by-default, top layer, light dismiss, Escape and focus return are the platform popover's own — what the app still owes is only which option is checked, kept by the screen's observer.

11 — the four themes are app-private tokens. Felt Emerald, Midnight Obsidian, Cyber Neon and Crimson Dragon retint the mat, frame and card backs through [data-theme] scopes over the app's own token names in a shared stylesheet. They never redeclare a platform token, so the design system keeps its single declaration and the light/dark twin stays orthogonal: a theme is a material, an appearance is the room.

12 — keep policy: arena 0, regras 1. The table is dealt on arrival, so holding a stale instance would show a hand that no longer exists; the rules screen is consulted mid-hand and holding it keeps the scroll position where the reader left it.

13 — no auth block. The terminal offers passkey and guest; this app declares neither, because nothing here is per-person and a sign-in gate on a bar game is a toll. The profile bar shows the seat — an avatar, the title the score earns, and the word convidado — and it is honest about being local.

14 — no hatches. Every screen uses the terminal’s declared bindings, handlers and renderers.

15 — one looping animation, and it is the stake. Cards lift, the mat settles, ticks stamp, the score counts — all transitions on the motion tokens, so reduced motion collapses them. The stake badge's pulse is the single loop in the app and it runs only while the hand is raised, which is the one state a player must not miss.

16 — the house plays correctly, not deviously. Lowest winning card, else lowest card; it accepts a raise when its hand is strong and runs when it is not. Bluffing and card counting are declined at this stage: an opponent that lies is only fun once the player trusts the table, and trust is built by an opponent whose moves can be checked against the rules screen.

18 — the board is a fixed viewport object, not a page. The arena occupies exactly the viewport minus the terminal's nav strip and never scrolls: a table you have to scroll is a web page, and the felt stops being an object the moment part of it is somewhere else. Every size inside is viewport-relative — the card is a fraction of the shorter side, the score figures and the spine scale with it — so a phone in portrait and a desktop across the room get the same board rather than two layouts. On a desktop it stops growing at a table's worth of space and sits in the middle of the room; on a phone it fills the screen.

19 — every screen rule is nested under its own screen. The terminal injects a screen's stylesheet into the document head and it outlives the screen that brought it, so a bare .mat rule reaches every screen that follows. This app has two screens sharing a material vocabulary, and the arena's grid tore the rules screen apart until both were nested under [data-screen]. The shared layer (shell/shared/table.css) stays unscoped on purpose: it is the vocabulary both screens speak.

17 — no sound. The brief does not ask for it, the terminal publishes no audio capability, and a card game that makes noise in a browser tab is a card game people mute. The settle and the pulse carry the feedback instead.

20 — nobody bids against themselves. The side that called truco cannot call the next rung: once its raise is accepted the right to climb belongs to the other side, and the call button says so rather than going quietly inert. Answering a raise with a higher one is still one gesture, and it is recorded as two — the rung on the table is accepted, then the next is called — because that is what the log has to be able to replay.

21 — the table keeps its cards. Played cards are not collected between tricks; each closed trick stays on the felt, smaller and dimmed, with the card that took it still lit. A player who looked away can read the whole hand off the table, which is what a real table gives free and a screen has to be told to do. It also retires the sweep animation an earlier pass had: the cards going somewhere was a poor substitute for the cards still being there.

32 — the hand plays itself out of its own rows, on the shared rules. The rules are truco_match's, the same chart and reducers online play runs in PostgreSQL. Pronto composes the arena's Jessie module (machines/arena.js) over them as one handler, and one fold seated on the table's row plays the game: it applies your intents, lets the house choose for every other seat from that seat's own view, and draws the felt from yours — rows in, updates out, no DOM and no clock. The game itself rides on the table's row, so a match replays out of its rows and a hand that broke is a repro rather than a story. A compartment has no randomness, so the one draw a game needs is asked for — the fold says it wants one and is called again with it — and kept on the row; every deal follows from it and the game's revision. The table plays without a clock: a turn waits for you, and the house's pauses are the terminal's (decision-30). Every value a row carries is written as text, because a region's filter compares a column against the text standing in the markup.

31 — whose turn it is is a fold over the log. A card may be tapped exactly when it is your turn to lay one, and that follows from the cards already laid: the rodada in play is the first without a verdict, its lead is whoever took the one before, and the seats fill it in order. So it is folded out of the log and written onto the hand, rather than stated by the dealer out of a model only the dealer can see — which is what made the screen right for only as long as that model was. Two facts no card states are carried instead: the round records who led its first rodada, and the match records how many seats are playing.

30 — the fold's clock is the terminal's, and it asks. A handler is evaluated in a compartment with nothing endowed and therefore has no clock, so a table that pauses between plays cannot time itself: it returns the pause it wants along with its writes, and the terminal calls it again when the pause is over. An earlier pass gave it a clock the other way round, as a CSS animation on the felt whose end arrived as an event; that worked, but it had to be exempted from the reduced-motion sweep — which collapses every animation to a millisecond — or the game ran at infinite speed for exactly the people who asked for calm. A pause the reduce asks for has no such problem, and the felt keeps no clock at all. Which animation ended is still carried on the event where one is: every animation inside a region bubbles to it, the terminal's own item arrivals included.

29 — the hands and the felt are rows, so the terminal can keep its nodes. The felt is three regions over the play log, one per rodada, so a card already down is never replaced under the eye and a rodada that closes keeps its own node — which is what lets the shrink it takes on be a transition of something already on the table rather than an element born small. A played card carries the angle it lies at and what it renders with for the same reason a held one does: a template binds fields and cannot compute. Every hand at the table is rows — one per card, carrying the seat that holds it. Yours is the one with a template: the cards persist across every unrelated redraw, and they are the only thing on the felt a player touches. The seats across from you are still drawn as backs, but from their rows rather than from a count, because an engine evaluated in a compartment has no memory between calls and a hand it cannot read is a hand it cannot play. So they are a tab-path entity read by a region that names a seat, and the terminal's keyed reconciliation owns their identity: item nodes persist across refreshes, patched rather than replaced. Drawn imperatively they were rebuilt on every store change, which replayed the deal animation on every move and left no node alive long enough to animate a card out of the hand. The row carries its rendered parts (rank, suit, face) because a template binds fields and cannot compute one from another.

22 — the table speaks the variant's words. In Paulista a trick is a vaza and the race to twelve is a partida; in Minas the trick is a rodada and the race to twelve is a jogo, and both call the three-trick deal a mão. Queda is neither, and it is not another word for jogo: a jogo is the race to twelve tentos, a queda is the best of three jogos — the side that takes two — and it is the tier above the ladder, never a rung on it. This app plays a single jogo and keeps no queda tally, so it does not use the word at all. The first pass had all three wrong (trick as queda, match as jogo) on nothing better than a guess; the owner sent it back and the sources settled it. The vocabulary follows the rules in force everywhere — the table's line, the rules screen, the log — because a Mineiro player reading "vaza" is being told the app learned the game somewhere else. Both words ship as real text with the inactive one removed, never as generated content: a sentence assembled out of ::before is a sentence nobody can select, search, or hear.

23 — the mão passes, and the pé is a position the table shows. The player who leads a hand is the mão; the last to play is the pé, and the pé has the final word on every trick of it — which is why a player wants to know which one they are before they choose a card. The lead had been fixed on the player, which is not the game: it passes one seat every hand, and the seat bar wears the position.

24 — the bot was bought, measured, and declined. Rather than reason out a better opponent, the obvious move was to take one off the shelf: npm:mcts (MIT, a hundred lines, four methods) wired to these rules, with the hidden hand handled by determinization — sample the unseen cards, search that perfect-information game, vote. Measured over the same 1,200 deals it lost: 44.5% at four samples, 46.3% at sixteen, against the shipped heuristic's 50%, at 22ms a hand. The reason is structural rather than a bad adapter: a truco hand is three cards, so the tree is too small for search to find anything the heuristic misses, while random playouts are noisier than a rule that is nearly right. The library stays out. Where a bot could still pay is the betting, and that is where the sweep below put its numbers.

25 — eles have names. "A casa" is a poker word: the house, the bank, the institution that cannot be beaten. Truco has no house — it has the other pair, who are as beatable as you are, which is why a truco scoreboard says NÓS and ELES and never "the house". The seats are renamed to match, and the opponents become a small cast (Seu Nezinho, Dona Cida, Tião Pandeiro, Zé da Bicicleta, and Bigode as the parça), each a set of betting thresholds around the swept centre with a face and a line. Each also has a tell that lands a beat before their call — the Punch-Out!! bargain, where the opponent tells you what is coming and it is your job to be looking. It moves the game's tension from chance to attention.

26 — the house's numbers are swept, not reasoned. The first pass rated a hand by mean card power, which one manilha dominates: (100, 1, 2) averages 34 and (10, 9, 8) averages 9, though the second hand wins far more often. Capping each card at "a card that reliably takes a trick" first ranks hands half again as well (r .54 against .39 over 30k deals). The thresholds on top of it were then swept rather than argued: pot odds say a hand worth 1 raised to 3 is worth taking above 33%, which is most hands — and playing it that way lost 0.058 tentos a hand, because the odds of a hand as dealt are not its odds against somebody who just raised. The swept pair (call at 8.0, take at 7.0) wins 0.016 instead, and the personas are that pair moved around.

27 — the app checks its own fit. The platform's visual battery measures tap targets, overlap and viewport bounds, and reported zero findings while the call button sat under the board and small type measured 1.0 against its background. Neither is a gap in its judgment: a control clipped by an overflow: hidden ancestor is inside the viewport, and the battery does not compute contrast at all. So the app declares tests/fit.ts at integrate — every control's box against the board's box, every piece of small type against what is actually behind it, across five window shapes and all four tables. It caught both regressions the same afternoon they were written, which is the argument for app-declared checks in general: the platform's battery is a floor, and an app with an unusual surface owes itself the rest.

33 — one rail that shows the live call, with the suspended one drawn above it. With flor, real envido and falta envido a first rodada can hold five callable things at once, on a phone, over the hand. Three layouts were drawn. Two rails give the second wager its own fixed strip in gold above the hand, which never surprises anyone and costs height in the one rodada where the hand matters most. Table talk puts each call beside whoever said it and keeps one small cluster of buttons, which is the truest to a bar table and the least conventional. One rail carries whichever call is live and draws the outstanding truco above it as a dashed strip that says what it is worth and that it is still waiting. The one rail wins on the thing the round was built around: a truco answered with envido leaves that truco standing, and this is the only one of the three where the player can see it standing rather than having to remember it. The mechanism and the picture agree. Its cost is real and accepted — the rail means different things at different moments, so the rail is labelled with the wager it is currently asking about, and the two wagers are told apart by colour rather than by position: truco keeps the ink of --primary, the envido family takes --accent, and flor takes --attention. Colour carries the distinction the fixed positions used to.

34 — a tie is laid side by side, and so is the rodada that breaks it. Cards played to a rodada land across each other, each at its own angle, because that is how they fall on a table. A tie is the exception players make by hand: the rodada that settles it is played by showing the highest card, and cards being compared are set down next to each other, square, rather than on top. Three treatments were shot on the real table: only the deciding rodada opened; the deciding rodada and the tie it settles both opened; and the deciding rodada stood upright with a lit edge, as a card held up to be seen. The second was chosen. Opening the tie as well means a finished hand reads off the felt as what happened — a tie, then the rodada that broke it — without the phase tracker. Only the look changes: the verdict, the order of play and the rules are the same.

28 — the card face is two corners and one mark, chosen at 53px. The face shipped as a corner rank with one large pip in the middle. Three faces were drawn and rendered at the three sizes the table actually uses — 53px (a short window), 69px (a phone), 117px (a desk): the shipped one, a real baralho with counted pips and lettered courts, and rank-and-suit in both corners with a single central mark. The counted pips lose at 53px, where six and seven are the same smudge, so they lose outright: one face has to serve every size. The two-corner face wins on a functional argument rather than a decorative one — a fanned hand shows nothing but the top-left corner, so rank and suit belong there together, and the shipped face put the suit in the middle where an overlapped card hides it. Court cards keep their letter: a drawn figure at 53px is a smudge too.

35 — opponent identity anchors to the north stage and scoreboard. Playing online against another person previously obscured who was sitting across the table: the opponent avatar had no nameplate or handle tag, the scoreboard bound an unmapped key that evaluated to empty, and the top bar displayed only the local player's handle, creating confusion about who was who. The opponent seat now owns its presence at the north stage with an explicit handle, online pill badge, role label, and monogram avatar disc. The scoreboard prints the opponent's actual name rather than a generic fallback. The local player's seat profile remains cleanly anchored in the seatbar with explicit attribution, keeping the felt actions completely unobstructed across all device viewports.

36 — an online match is played at the arena, and the bar is only where players meet. A page of its own for an online table made two games of one: the match against a friend looked and felt nothing like the match against the house, and accepting a challenge sent you somewhere else. The arena now seats the match itself: its fold reads your seat's view from truco_match_seat and draws it as it draws the house's game, from whichever seat is yours, and a gesture is a truco_match_command row keyed by the revision it was made at. What a player says at the table is not the match's: each player has a truco_voice, opened the first time a match seats them, which sits at the table under the name they go by there and counts their shouts, so the same word twice is two shouts. Its public row is what the other seats read — the opponent's name and what they shout — so a table joined by its code knows its players as well as one a challenge dealt, and a shout never moves the match's revision under a card in flight. A voice sits only at a seat the match gave its player, which the database checks against the match's members. A shout carried by the match itself was the first cut; it made every shout race the game, and names reached only tables a challenge had dealt. A voice per table was weighed too, but Mecha seats an instance's members when it is spawned, and a table filled by its code fills one join at a time. A code typed in the bar asks to join its table from the arena; a table still waiting keeps the bar open, which shows its code.

37 — every online game starts at a table, and the bar is where its chairs fill. A match against people needs two, four or six of them, and the bar had one gesture for one of them. Now a table is the one thing: you open it as 1v1, 2v2 or 2v2v2, kept on the bar's list or reserved for its code and your calls, and it deals when its last chair fills. A chair is a pair's before anyone sits in it — the one across from you is your partner's, and of three pairs each sits across from itself — so the chair you call someone to is the pair they play for, and there is no team to pick. A call is a truco_call: the admission refuses a chair taken or already called, and answering it is the target's join to that chair in the same gesture, so a chair taken meanwhile refuses the join and nothing else. The bar's list is the match's public projection, which a reserved table never publishes; and since a room is its table's code, what anyone reads of a table names it by its talk, a draw of its opener's (or its inviter's, for one a challenge deals) that its seats are shown and its room is not derived from. A chair at a table still waiting is held at one table at a time — the match refuses a join from anyone seated at another that waits — so no one keeps chairs from the players who would fill them. A guest stands up from a table still waiting, giving its chair back; its opener closes it. A talk names one table, so a table or challenge opened with one already in use is refused. A challenge stays the one-gesture way to a game of two, and plays 1v1 under the arena's rules — the mineiro's where those are the three pairs' own. The table is a boteco's — square, checkered cloth, the deck and its vira — and three pairs sit around a longer one.

Historical design reviews

These reviews record the original assembly. Current contracts are defined above and in the source.

backend — the machine

Verdict: signed, with two conditions carried into the program.

  • The three entities are the brief's three, and the path assignment is uniform and cheap. No access model is written and none is meaningful: a tab row is private by construction, and the schema refuses access on that path, so the security surface here is genuinely empty rather than merely unwritten.
  • No pipeline means no idempotence argument and no loop to break — the publication-scoping hazard that has bitten every cluster-tier app in this repo cannot arise. That is the strongest argument for decision-01 and it is worth stating: this app is immune to the platform's most expensive bug class because it declined the plane it lives on.
  • Condition 1: the dealer writes through forms (decision-03), and a form field's value is a string. Integer fields — scores, stake, vaza, power — must be read back as numbers by whatever compares them, or the ladder will compare "12" with 9 and get the wrong answer. The program owes an explicit coercion at the dealer's read boundary, once, not at each comparison.
  • Condition 2: ids are minted by the dealer rather than defaulted by the store, because tab emits no DDL and therefore no gen_random_uuid(). Every create form carries an id field for that reason; a create that omits it is a bug the store cannot catch for us. They are derived rather than drawn: a key is built out of what it identifies, so writing the same row twice is writing the same row, and a seeded sitting replays down to its ids. The match is the exception and the root — nothing stands above it to derive from, so it is the one draw a sitting makes.
  • Bijection: three entities, two screens, ten states, ten flows, twenty-one tests, seventeen decisions, no handler, no hatch. The dealer deliberately carries no ir id because no CUE object realizes it — that is a known gap in the bijection's coverage, filed by the platform advisor below rather than papered over here.

frontend — the built surface

Verdict: signed, with three findings to hold at assembly.

  • The screen count is right: one table and one reference. The arena carries ten forms, which is a lot of markup for one screen, but nine of them are hidden field sets with no visible chrome — the visible surface is a hand, three buttons and three pickers.
  • Finding 1 (open): the custom dropdown (decision-10) is the app reimplementing a platform affordance, which the prelude warns against by name. It is accepted here because the brief makes the OS popup a product defect, but the assembly owes what the native control gives free: a real button trigger with aria-expanded, rows as buttons in a list, Escape to close, click-outside to close, and focus returning to the trigger. Anything less and this is a worse control, not a prettier one.
  • Finding 2 (open): the cards in hand are drawn by the dealer, not by a region, so they are the one part of the screen the storybook cannot render from fixtures. The frames above are therefore the only review surface for the hand's geometry. The assembly must keep card markup structurally identical whether the dealer or a fixture wrote it, so the visual battery measures the real thing.
  • Finding 3 (advisory, still open): the screen states include five the terminal does not drive (dealt collapsed into populated, plus raised, resolved, match-over, picking). The terminal's own vocabulary — form-submit, validation-error, network-error — is not reachable in an app whose store is local and whose forms never refuse, so those states are styled to the same rest appearance rather than left unstyled; an unstyled state is invisible until the day it fires.
  • CSS spends the design tokens and declares only app-private names for the felt, wood, stock and rim (decision-11) — the shared layer keeps its single declaration and check-screens can prove it.

designer — the system

Verdict: signed. DESIGN.md is comparative, the token set is complete in both appearances, and the frames hold.

  • The identity — Brasa & Feltro — comes from a landscape read of the four products the brief names plus the design bar it cites, with what is borrowed and what is refused recorded per product. The table-as-object direction won over two alternatives that were drawn and rejected: a flat card-grid (reads as a settings screen), and a neon-first arcade look (Cyber Neon is one theme, not the identity — an identity that is already at maximum has nowhere to go when the stake rises).
  • Colour carries meaning and nothing else: brass is hardware, ember is the stake, red is the refusal. Because gold appears only on metal, the manilha crown and the active dropdown row read instantly; because red appears only on danger, the twelve rung needs no animation to land.
  • Both appearances are first-class and every token has a twin. The felt themes are appearance-independent by construction (decision-11), which is what keeps four themes ×two appearances from being eight palettes to maintain.
  • Frames redrawn (closed). The board became a fixed viewport object mid-build (decision-18) and the sketches went on depicting the page it had been — a storyboard that describes something else is worse than none, because it is signed. Every arena frame is redrawn against what ships: the HUD row with its pickers, the score pair with the stake between them, the spine wearing its verdicts, the vira line, and the felt holding the tricks it has kept.
  • Frames: the hierarchy holds at 300×420 — score, stake, spine, mat, hand, actions — and the unhappy frames (raised, match-over) are drawn, not implied. Finding (advisory): the resolved frame's three vaza chips repeat information the spine already carries; at assembly they should differ in kind (verdict, not progress) or one of them goes.
  • No AI-slop patterns: no gradient-on-everything, no glassmorphism, no emoji-as-icon-system, no purple-blue default. The one gradient in the app is the mahogany trigger, and it is a material.

ux — the felt experience

Verdict: signed, with one question answered against the brief and one cut.

  • The journey is one gesture deep: open, and a hand is dealt with a legal move available. No sign-in, no onboarding, no empty state — the first frame is a game in progress (accept-instant-table). That is the whole product promise and the screen count protects it.
  • Gesture cost per hand: three taps to play a hand out, one more to raise, one to answer. Nothing needs two taps that could need one, and the only modal moment in the app — a raise awaiting an answer — is the one moment where proceeding without answering would be meaningless.
  • The question the brief did not answer: what happens on a tie, and what a refused raise pays. Both are now stated in the brief's Behavior section and drawn on the rules screen, because a truco table where the rules are ambiguous is a table where the loser thinks the app cheated.
  • Cut: a per-hand history screen. The log region on the arena answers "what just happened" during the hand, which is when the question is asked; a separate screen for finished hands is a place nobody would stand. Recorded here so it is a decision rather than an omission.
  • Unhappy roads are designed: the match-over frame offers two doors rather than a dead end, and a raise the player ignores is not possible — the answer buttons are the only live controls in that state.
  • Three findings from the product owner, mid-build, all upheld (decision-20, 21, 22): a player could raise their own bet, which no rulebook allows; the cards were collected between tricks, so looking away lost the hand; and Mineiro was being made to say "vaza". This seat had signed a flow set that hid all three — the lesson it takes is that a rules-carrying app needs a player of the game at the table before the flows are signed, not after the storyboard is drawn.
  • Finding (open): the house answering "within a beat" (the brief's words) is a real timing contract and the assembly owes it — a beat long enough to read as a decision, short enough that the table never feels stalled. It is the one place in the app where a delay is a feature, and it must still collapse under reduced motion.

qa — the evidence

Verdict: signed, and the verify evidence is now on the record rather than owed.

Evidence, from the loop's own verbs. just lint: bijection [], cue vet clean, check-screens []. just integrate runs both declared checks: the visual battery reports 0 critical / 0 advisory across two routes × three viewports, and tests/acceptance.ts — declared as an integrate check on the loop seat, so a verb reaches it — reports 16/16 against the running cluster with a clean console. The storybook renders 9/9 arena frames with posed hands and no errors.

  • Coverage runs both ways: thirty acceptance ids, every one cited by a test or a storyboard path, and every data-accepts value resolves. The rules half of the app is the easy half to prove — power, manilhas, verdicts and the ladder are pure functions and their CEL is decidable against injected bindings.
  • Falsifiability: each invariant is written so a plausible wrong implementation fails it. test-run-scores pins the exact bug a run rule usually has (paying the raised value instead of the pre-raise one); test-vaza-tie pins all three tie branches rather than asserting "ties are handled".
  • Finding 1 (closed). test-offline and test-touch-and-motion are properties of the running app, not of a pure function. Both are now observed: the driver counts every request a full hand makes (zero that are not the terminal's own sync plane, which a tab-path app never opens), and the visual battery measures every tap target at three viewports. The one regression this caught was ours — a compacted nav strip took its links to 21px — which is the argument for measuring rather than judging.
  • Finding 2 (closed). Randomness arrives as an input: without a seed the table deals from the platform's generator, and ?seed= replays a table exactly — same deal, same nerve from the house, since both draw from the one source. The driver asserts it both ways (one seed twice is one hand; two seeds are two), and it earned its keep immediately: the hand that froze mid-trick was diagnosed on a seed that reproduced it three runs out of three.
  • Finding 3 (closed). The storybook renders from fixtures and the hand is dealer-drawn, so the frames would have reviewed the chrome and not the cards. The dealer poses instead of playing when it detects the storybook, drawing the same card markup the table draws: 9/9 frames render a hand, and the review surface covers the part of the screen the store cannot describe.
  • Finding 5 (closed by a check). Two regressions shipped that every automated surface called clean: the call button pushed under the board when the cards grew, and text at 1.0 contrast on the new cloth. The owner found both by looking. tests/fit.ts now measures exactly those two properties across five window shapes and four tables (decision-27) — and the second one only became computable when the instruments moved onto opaque paper, because a translucent strip over a gradient stack has no background any checker can name.
  • Finding 4 (open, and the sharpest one). Four of this compile's five real bugs were found by the driver or by the product owner, not by the invariants: hidden form values leaking between submissions, a screen's timers outliving its DOM, two matches claiming to be the playing one, and a second tap landing a third card in a two-seat trick. Every one is a property of the app in flight — of ordering, lifetime and repetition — and the ir's CEL vocabulary can only speak about a single call's input and output. This seat does not have a fix to propose; it has a gap to name, because the tests that would have caught these are not tests this ir can currently express.

platform advisor — reading across the five

Advisory, no gate. Three observations about the seams.

  • This app uses the platform by declining most of it. No cluster plane is switched on: no auth, no blobs, no pipelines, no policies, no migrations. That is not underuse — tab is a published durability rung and the brief chose it — but it does mean the compile exercises the terminal almost alone, and the terminal is the half of the platform with nine concerns and no substrate. Expect the friction here to be in the shell, not the store.
  • The dealer is the platform's shape showing through. The terminal's residual-computation story is a pure reduce with one event source (drag). A tap-driven engine has no seat at that table, so the app grew one. Two honest readings: either the handler contract needs a second event source and a way to describe writes as form submissions — which is what the dealer does by hand — or apps of this kind are outside pronto's declared scope and the brief should have been refused. This seat's read is the former, and it notes that the terminal has now grown the mechanism that makes the dealer possible without growing the contract that would make it reviewable: it carries no ir id, so the bijection — the checker that exists precisely to police arbitrary code — cannot see it. That is the single largest gap in this compile.
  • Measured against its siblings. Counted from source, this app is the outlier in one dimension only: 1,206 lines of assembly per screen, against 308 for xpense, 324 for realworld and 150 for thenote. That number is the dealer plus the table's materials — the price of an app whose subject the declarative surface does not cover — and it is worth knowing that the price is concentrated rather than spread: the entity count (3) and the program (633 lines) are the smallest of the four. It is also the only app that carries its own tests (627 lines) and the only one with ten declared checks; both follow from the same fact.
    Measured at runtime, the tab path delivers what it promises: the table is playable ~700ms after the request and makes zero requests and zero bytes sitting idle for ten seconds — no shape stream, no poll, nothing to keep alive. The comparison against a crud-path app could not be completed: xpense would not get past its sign-in gate in this harness.
  • The siblings do not boot. Trying to measure one turned up a repo-wide fact worth more than the benchmark: xpense's checked-in emission predates jessie.js joining the interpreter's file list, so its baked image lacks the file, the shell 404s, and the app never mounts. Regenerating it (write.ts, one command, no source change) fixes it — which means the emitted tree silently rots against the platform between compiles, and nothing in the loop notices until somebody launches the app. A generated tree that is checked in needs a check that it is still what the generator would write.
  • Where the seams were used rather than worked around: forms-only mutations survived (decision-03) even for machine writes, which is the difference between an escape hatch and a second platform; the design system kept one declaration under four themes (decision-11); and the app declares no capability the terminal does not offer. Where it was worked around: the custom dropdown reimplements a control the terminal publishes, for a product reason the design seat argued and this seat accepts — with the accessibility bill the frontend seat has already itemized.
